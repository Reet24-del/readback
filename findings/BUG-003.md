# BUG-003: Indian DD/MM dates are read in US order. 03/04/2026 becomes "March fourth"

| | |
|---|---|
| **Status** | Candidate. Found by the verbatim transcription; not yet confirmed by ear. |
| **Severity** | High: appointments, due dates and dates of birth come out as the wrong day |
| **Engine / voice** | Voice Agent API TTS via `greeting`, voice `anna` (en-GB) |
| **Reproducibility** | 9 of 9: all three ambiguous numeric dates on all three voices (anna en-GB, alba and george en-US). Unambiguous formats were correct. |

## Evidence (verbatim transcription)
| Written | Means (India) | Spoken as |
|---|---|---|
| `03/04/2026` | 3 April 2026 | **March fourth**, twenty twenty six ❌ |
| `12/11/2026` | 12 November 2026 | **December eleventh**, twenty twenty six ❌ |
| `01.02.1990` | 1 February 1990 | **January second**, nineteen ninety ❌ |
| `31-12-2026` | 31 December 2026 | thirty one twelve twenty twenty six (no month name) |
| `2026-10-24` | 24 October 2026 | the twenty fourth of October, twenty twenty six ✅ |
| `5 Jan 2027` | 5 January 2027 | the fifth of January twenty twenty seven ✅ |

## Why it matters
India (and most of the world) writes dates as DD/MM/YYYY. The voice here is British English, where DD/MM is also standard, yet it uses the US MM/DD reading. A patient told their appointment is "March fourth" instead of 3 April misses it by a month. "01.02.1990" read as "January second" gives the wrong date of birth in an identity check.

A locale setting (or simply following the voice's own locale) would fix this. When a date is ambiguous, reading out the digits is safer than guessing the order.

## Reproduce
```bash
.venv/bin/python -m readback.runner --ids date-001,date-002,date-008,date-003 --voices anna
```
